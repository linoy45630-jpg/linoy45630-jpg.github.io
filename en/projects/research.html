Who? How? Under What Conditions?

Qualitative research on how special education professionals decide whether and how to integrate generative AI (GenAI) into their teaching. Submitted to the Chais Conference for the Study of Innovation and Learning Technologies.

Authors
Linoy Kahalon, Regina Avraham
Participants
15 special education professionals
Method
Semi-structured interviews, thematic analysis
Context
Seminar, HIT

Why this topic

We were asked to choose a topic that connects learning and technology. We deliberately chose an under-researched area: how special education teachers decide whether, and how, to use AI.

We also chose a fully qualitative approach, the only group in our class to do so. We wanted to look beyond the numbers. Not another yes-or-no survey or a 1-to-5 scale, but hearing the people themselves: how they think, what worries them and what matters to them.

My part

  • Participant recruitment: finding and reaching educators who met the criteria, through professional contacts, educator groups and referrals, all while on active reserve duty.
  • Writing the chapters: literature review, methodology, findings and discussion.

Key findings

What AI offers

Creating and adapting teaching materials, meeting different learning levels, connecting to students' interests, accessibility and less workload.

What gets in the way

Inaccurate or unsuitable outputs, repeated prompting, limited infrastructure and gaps in training.

What decides

Beyond usefulness: each student's needs, privacy, independent thinking, human connection and the teacher's personal voice.

Integration is described as an ongoing process of evaluation and adaptation, not a one-time decision to adopt or reject technology.

What I took from it

In special education, an effective tool isn't necessarily the right tool.

The research reinforces a principle I work by: before choosing a technology, understand the specific learner and the person who will teach with it.

Abstract

This study examined how special education professionals describe their decisions about selecting and integrating generative AI into teaching. Using semi-structured interviews with 15 educators and inductive thematic analysis, it found that decisions extend beyond perceived usefulness and ease of use, to individual student needs, privacy, independent thinking and the teacher's personal voice.
